After the Games Industry

15 theses on post-industrial playful media.

Sean Taylor · October 2026

I. The Turn II. The Shape III. The Practice

Act I: The Turn

  1. The industrial era of games is over.

    Empires are dying under the weight of fixed costs and low returns. Breakouts are rarer while a handful of evergreen titles hoover up attention and revenue. Headcounts are shrinking, studios are shutting, development is deprofessionalising and distribution has been thrown wide open. What remains is not a smaller, wiser version of the industrial model. It's a post-industrial ecosystem where big teams and antiquated pipelines give way to cheaper tools, new routes to market and a sharper fight for attention.

  2. Distribution is infinite. Attention is scarce.

    The great inversion. The industrial problem was getting onto the shelf. Now it's getting into someone's day. The hours haven't changed, but the things competing for them have. Every assumption built on distribution scarcity is now wrong, and so is every assumption that ignores the new kingmakers: algorithms, creators, curators and communities.

  3. The middle of the road is dead on arrival.

    Attention and money pull to the edges: high-production spectacle, high-signal art, DIY weirdness, and pop monoculture. The middle is too expensive for word-of-mouth and too square for people who care. There is no market for 5/10.

  4. Everything is a games platform now.

    Wordle habits, Duolingo streaks, Strava heatmaps, interactive trailers, prediction markets, gambling apps, dopamine simulators, parasocial playthings. Leisure that wants retention has lifted the mechanics and left the game behind. Most non-game derivatives win in a shallow attention fight. What they can't easily copy is intensity, unfakeable identity and affinity for an experience. That's what's left to play for.

  5. Attention is your banker. Platforms are your landlords.

    Games are often not the business. They are the engagement engine that makes someone else's ecosystem more valuable. So the question isn't "can this game make money?" but "whose rails does this run on, and what do they take?" Selling a product, feeding a platform and creating collateral are different strategies. Most studios still treat them as interchangeable. Direct relationships (owned community, direct payment, portable identity) are the only durable counter to landlord economics.

Act II: The Shape

  1. Games are dissolving into amusements.

    The opposite of work is play, not games. Amusements are the bigger category: personalised, contextual, participatory playthings across media, software, commerce, sport and culture. A sports-fan daily play format isn't competing with EA Sports FC. It's competing with Strava, TikTok and Polymarket. And it's competing on behavioural efficiency: speed of reward, meaningful progress and visible participation.

  2. Slop is what high-status incumbents call low-status leisure.

    From the inside, it's texture, companionship, belonging, drama, play and a place to be. The incumbent mistake is arguing about their own taste while the audience votes with its time and money.

  3. Participation is the product.

    The atomic unit of value has shifted from the finished game to active participation. Play is one form. Watching, clipping, predicting, collecting, remixing, backing, and belonging are others. The design challenge goes beyond getting people to play. It's giving the lurker, the regular and the obsessive each something to do.

  4. Every plaything is an attention engine that leaks.

    Attention gets people in. Engagement gives them reasons to stay. The engine turns attention into participation, participation into affinity and affinity into patronage. The top 20% of players account for roughly 60% of playtime and 73% of spend (Bain, 2026). Most games don't have a content problem. They leak attention before it deepens into affinity.

  5. Feel is the floor. Status is the moat.

    How it feels - and how it makes people feel - decides whether anyone stays past first contact. Anticipation decides whether they return. Status decides whether switching costs them something social. Play: a core loop that makes the first interaction worthwhile. Progress: a reason to return. Participate: identity, expression and recognition, in the game and around it. People don't just play. They signal.

  6. Your IP is now an API.

    The world is the durable asset; the product is a pop-up inside it. Let fans, partners and agents build on it and play with it. Don't take worldbuilding literally, but do take it seriously. Lore, aesthetics, status signals and social codes are what turn music scenes, meme machines and cult communities into places people want to spend time. Give people the codes, tools and permission to participate. Even an API needs direction.

Act III: The Practice

  1. People, ideas, machines - in that order.

    Machines got cheap, then generative. Making is a solved problem. What remains scarce is judgement: who it's for, why they'd care, what's worth making and what turns attention into affinity. Cheaper machines do not just spin the loop faster. They make poor judgement catastrophically expensive.

  2. The audience is evidence.

    Usage is oxygen for ideas. Get out of the building. Maker mode lies: it overweights what makers like and underweights what people actually do. Small, fast probes into real desires beat large, slow bets on internal conviction. Trust what people return to and cluster around, not what they say they want.

  3. Orchestration beats authorship.

    Create the conditions for value to circulate. Fewer cathedrals, more chaos containers. Fewer polished products, more participatory probes. Authorship still matters at the seed. After that, the work is gardening, not steering: you don't decide what grows, you decide what gets watered.

  4. Culture and collateral beat commodities.

    The thing is not always the thing. The customer is not always the customer. Commodities compete on price and convenience. Culture and collateral compete on identity and affinity. Capture value through mementos, memberships, status, and access that feel like belonging, not extraction. Money becomes meaningful when it expresses belief.
