The anchors

Before we built anything, we defined what it had to feel like.

The Denki Difference defined our in-house design principles: the End meant thinking like a player; the Way meant thinking like a maker. We codified what the Quarrel pilot proved into ten Guiding Lights beneath the End, giving us a shared vision and a clear test for every production decision.

Pre-production

We rebuilt it from the ground up, one playable slice at a time.

Our 40/20/40 production model still held. On a longer timeline, pre-production became a series of iteration loops, each checked against the End before the next began.

20%
40%
Pre-production: loopsProductionPost-production

A board game first. We rebuilt Quarrel as a board game with full rules so we could prototype cheaply, test quickly and refine relentlessly. Each day was a sprint around a critical risk or design flaw. Prototypes took minutes. We kept what worked and killed the rest.

Opponents played in character. The team roleplayed each artificial opponent as a player archetype (Impulsive, Cautious, Bully, Coward) until players could recognise its personality through its decisions.

Words you know. And words you discover. We tagged every word in Quarrel’s dictionary by familiarity: about 6,000 most people know, 30,000 some people know, and the remaining 75% few would recognise. Every word came with its official Collins definition, so players could discover new words as well as play the ones they knew.

06 / 11

The production model

Every day, every week, every milestone: a short loop back to the End.

I built the production around short feedback loops, so everyone always had clarity of purpose, plans, progress and priority.

  • Daily. Start with stand-ups. End with dailies: the day’s work on screen, like rushes on a film set.
  • Weekly. A build that delivers a meaningful new slice of the game. Then show-and-tell: everyone shares what they made, how they’re feeling and what they’re thinking.
  • Monthly milestones. Critical progress points with a shared definition of done, demonstrated on target hardware to key stakeholders, then the whole studio. In one milestone sprint in 2009, the build went from version 28 to version 53, with about 450 tasks completed.
  • Public playtesting. Getting out of the building and putting the game in front of strangers, without explanation, to see whether it upholds the End. At ProtoPlay in Edinburgh, 2009, almost 200 people played over three days. Most stayed for more than 30 minutes; many returned with their families and friends.

“Someone going out of their way to come back with their wife and kids for a multiplayer game of Quarrel is worth ten filled-in questionnaires.”

— Colin Anderson, 2009
07 / 11

Into the unknown

Three people became ten. A games-for-TV studio learned a console.

Almost everything about Quarrel was new to us: a bigger team, a new platform, online multiplayer and Xbox certification.

The team. Three coders, three artists, a designer, QA, the Creative Director and me. I built the team and the conditions for delivery: clear priorities, adaptive plans, fast decisions and the autonomy to act.

The platform. We took a studio built around work-for-hire TV games into original IP on Xbox Live Arcade, building around the platform’s strengths, with four-player online multiplayer at the heart of the experience.

Certification. With a small team and a single QA tester, we planned for Xbox certification from day one. We built it into production and passed with minimal friction or delay.

The production repertoire was transferable; the new platform was teachable.Work backwards from what’s right for the project, not what you already know.

10 / 11

What transfers

Protect the creative vision. Build the conditions to deliver it.

My role is to turn creative ambition into a game the team can deliver and players want to play. On Quarrel, that meant five production principles:

  1. Make the vision actionable. Turn creative direction into shared priorities and clear criteria for decisions.
  2. Build production around the project. Choose the team, tools and approach the game needs. Adapt the plan as you learn.
  3. Resolve risk early. Test the biggest uncertainties quickly and cheaply. Plan for dependencies, platform requirements and certification from the start.
  4. Make progress visible. Use playable builds and short feedback loops to expose problems, enable decisions and keep the team aligned.
  5. Define quality through the player’s experience. Set acceptance criteria around what players can do and feel. Test whether the game delivers on its promise.

Keep the End clear.Adapt the Way.Deliver on the promise.
